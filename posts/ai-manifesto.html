A Manifesto: My 33 Million Problems With the AI Revolution

My contemporary beef with AI

June 5, 2026

I took time off and traveled — through Central America, South America, to Patagonia. Somewhere in there I picked up sailing and started running ultramarathons. It was a brutal stretch of life. But it built something — a bias toward learning by doing and a deep skepticism of the view from the top.

I started in enterprise. Microsoft. Accenture. I learned how data moves at scale in enterprises, how decisions get made when resources are not a constraint, and what it looks like when a well-funded organization gets the full weight of modern technology behind it. Then I moved to the frontier — a YC-backed robotics startup, then a geometric deep learning company building AI for physical machines. I was inside the data layer: pipelines, annotation schemas, QA infrastructure. Close to the edge of what AI can currently do. The work was real — egocentric video pipelines, annotation schemas, QA frameworks for manipulation tasks. Frontier stuff. The kind of work that ends up in research papers and funding announcements.

But the longer I stayed at the frontier, the more I kept thinking about everyone who would never benefit from it.

The AI being built is extraordinarily powerful. It is also being built almost exclusively for organizations that already have the infrastructure to absorb it. The assumption baked into every product, every pitch, every funding announcement is that your business already has engineers, APIs, and the operational maturity to plug into something new. That assumption eliminates most of the economy before the conversation even starts.

I started paying attention to the businesses nobody was paying attention to. The ones running on spreadsheets and email chains and institutional knowledge locked inside one person's head. Not because they were behind — but because they were thriving without any of it. Decades of expertise. Real customer relationships. Deep domain knowledge.

Here is the problem: the economics of compute do not scale down. Enterprises have the engineers, the capital, and the infrastructure to absorb what modern AI demands. Elon Musk is building data centers in space. The resources required to stay competitive are compounding faster than most businesses can afford. The gap is not closing. It is accelerating.

SMBs will fall behind. Not because they are slow. Because nobody is building for them with any urgency. The pitch coming out of Silicon Valley is always the same: migrate to our platform, connect your APIs, retrain your staff. For a business with six employees and twenty years of process baked into muscle memory, that is not a solution. That is a threat.

There are roughly 33 million small businesses in the United States. They employ 46% of the private sector workforce and account for nearly 44% of economic activity. Vertical SaaS touches a fraction of them — Toast for restaurants, ServiceTitan for home services, Mindbody for fitness studios. It works when a business is large enough to justify the migration cost, has staff willing to retrain, and fits neatly into a mold someone else designed.

Most do not fit that mold. The long tail of truly idiosyncratic, relationship-driven, deeply manual businesses — the ones where the process lives in one person's head and has for thirty years — is exactly what vertical SaaS skips over.

It is not a niche. It is most of the economy.

The conversation is always about enterprise transformation, white collar productivity, frontier research. The 33 million small businesses that collectively employ half the country are treated as an afterthought — too fragmented, too hard to reach, too resistant to change.

That fragmentation is not the problem. That fragmentation is the opportunity.

I thought about hacking on it on the side. Building tools, pitching solutions, showing up at small businesses with something to offer. I actually tried this — during my time collecting egocentric data I offered to identify operational inefficiencies for free. People told me to get lost. Not because the problem wasn't real. Because trust has to come before technology. You cannot walk into a business that has operated for thirty years and lead with automation. They will show you the door.

I thought I understood the resistance. I had built AI integration plans. I had mapped workflows. I had a framework. Then I got closer and realized the problem goes deeper than I thought. It is not about tooling. It is not about cost. It is about trust — and there is still more I do not understand yet.

So the question became: how do you actually get inside the problem?

I realized the only way to understand the problem was going to be the Texas tried and true classic way to get your hands dirty.

So I recently accepted a role at one of San Francisco's most established rare coin and bullion dealers to lift their operations. The reason coins and numismatics is simple — I have collected since childhood. A hobby not even my closest friends know about. And I am taking on an automation project at my sailing school on the side, a handshake deal since they taught me how to sail.

Not as a technical co-founder. Not with an engineering team. Not with people who know what a kanban board is or have ever sat in a daily standup. I will be working with long-standing operations that have survived decades of business cycles in one of the most aggressive and volatile economies in the United States.

People have asked me if I am worried about getting left behind in technology by stepping away from robotics and SaaS. It is a fair question.

I was asked something similar when I decided to travel from Belize to Patagonia. And the honest answer then was yes — it terrified me.

My answer has always been the same: if it does leave me behind, then good. Technology was not for me and it should leave me behind.

Next Steps

Witter Coin is one of the most respected rare coin and bullion dealers in the country. Decades of expertise. Deep industry relationships.

Spinnaker Sailing SF has been teaching people to sail in the Bay for years. Memberships, instructors, boat logistics, student communications. Almost entirely by hand.

These are not failing businesses. They are thriving ones — completely untouched by the last decade of operational technology. They represent millions of businesses that form the backbone of the economy.

I am embedded inside both of them right now. Not as a consultant with a deck. As someone doing the work — mapping every manual process from the inside, automating what can be automated, building a playbook for how AI actually reaches the shop floor and the marina. Two different industries, same fundamental problem: expertise trapped in analog systems that have never been asked to talk to each other.

The playbook I am building is not theoretical. Every workflow I automate is a prototype. Every hour saved is a data point. Every skeptical employee who comes around after seeing it work is a lesson in how to actually deploy this in the real world.

The robots can wait.
